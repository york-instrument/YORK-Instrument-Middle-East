    Method

    The sample travels through the tube as a plug. Its transport time is the internal volume of the line divided by the actual volumetric flow:

    t = (π/4 · d² · L) / Qactual,   Qactual = Qnormal · (Patm / P) · (T / 273.15 K)

    Pressure matters: at 7 bar(g) the same normal flow occupies one eighth of the volume, so gas moves eight times slower than at atmospheric pressure. Reduce the pressure at the sample point when you can.

    Filters, regulators and the sensor cell behave like mixed volumes. Each adds an exponential response with time constant τ = V / Q; the tool adds 3τ for 95 % and 4.6τ for 99 %.

    Ways to speed up a slow line

    • Use a bypass (fast loop) so most of the flow is vented before the analyzer and only a small sample enters the cell.
    • Use the smallest tubing that the flow and pressure drop allow.
    • Reduce the pressure close to the sample point.
    • Keep filters and dead volumes small.

    For moisture this is only part of the story. Tubing walls adsorb and release water, so a dry-down after a change can take far longer than the transport time. Stainless steel with a clean, smooth bore dries down fastest.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do I calculate sample line lag time?

    Divide the internal volume of the tube by the actual flow at line pressure and temperature. For example, 20 m of 1/4" tubing (4.57 mm ID) holds about 330 mL; at 1 NL/min, atmospheric pressure and 25 °C the gas takes about 18 seconds to arrive.

    Does line pressure change the response time?

    Yes. At higher pressure the gas is compressed, so the same normal flow moves more slowly through the tube. Transport time grows roughly in proportion to absolute pressure.

    What is a fast loop or bypass?

    A bypass draws a larger flow through the sample line and vents most of it just before the analyzer. The line flushes faster while the analyzer still receives its normal flow.
